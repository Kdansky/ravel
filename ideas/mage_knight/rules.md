# Mage Knight Board Game — Rules

This is the WizKids/Vlaada Chvátil card-and-hex board game (2011, "Mage
Knight Board Game", reprinted 2019 as "Mage Knight: Ultimate Edition").
**Not** the earlier WizKids Mage Knight *miniatures* skirmish game, which
shares a publisher and a name and nothing else.

**Sources.** No single canonical rulebook was fetchable in full (the
official WizKids/CGE PDFs and the mageknight.net walkthrough PDF both
exceeded what could be pulled through the tools available this session).
This document is built from cross-checked secondary sources instead:
[UltraBoardGames' rules pages](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/mage-knight/game-rules.php)
(rules text, game elements, combat, tile revelation — the primary source
for most of this document, and the closest thing to a full rules
paraphrase reachable here), BoardGameGeek forum threads on specific
questions (Source dice count, reputation effects, resistance rules,
assault/garrison mechanics, cooperative/dummy-player mechanics), and the
game's Wikipedia page for basic framing. Every section below is written
from those; nothing is from unaided recollection. Where a specific number
could not be cross-confirmed across at least two independent hits, it is
flagged **[approximate]** — verify against the physical rulebook or
`boardgamegeek.com/filepage/75296` (Complete Rules Reference) before
building anything that depends on the exact figure.

## 1. Overview

1–4 players, each controlling one Hero, explore and fight across a hex
map that is built tile-by-tile as the game is played rather than laid out
complete at setup. The game is scenario-driven: a scenario book (11
scenarios in the base game **[approximate — count confirmed by one
source, not cross-verified]**) sets round limits, starting tiles, and win
conditions, and covers competitive, cooperative and solo play with the
same rules engine. Player interaction in the competitive scenarios is
almost entirely indirect — race to fame, race to conquer, share a map —
there is no card-passing, bidding or negotiation between players at any
point; it is closer to solitaire-played-in-parallel than to a
negotiation game.

Each Hero has a 16-card personal "Deed deck" (their own basic actions)
that they build up over the game with cards recruited from a shared
offer. There is no opposing deck and no player-vs-player card
interaction — the "deckbuilder" comparison this repo's `ideas/` files
make to Puzzle Strike and Lost Ruins of Arnak is about deck construction
during play, not about attacking another player's deck.

## 2. Round structure: day and night

A full game is **6 rounds**, alternating **3 Day rounds and 3 Night
rounds** (a scenario may run fewer rounds; the introductory scenario
"First Reconnaissance" runs 3 rounds, sourced as Day–Night–Day). A
two-sided Day/Night board tracks which is current and is flipped at the
round boundary. Day and Night are not cosmetic — they gate which mana
colors are freely usable (§4) and change specific terrain movement costs
(forests cost more to enter by night, deserts cost less).

**Each round**, in order:

1. Every player shuffles their Deed deck and draws a fresh hand of 5
   cards (hand size scales with Hero level, see §7).
2. Every player privately picks one **Tactic card** from that round's
   deck (a separate deck for Day and for Night, six cards each,
   confirmed via two sources). Tactics are revealed simultaneously.
   Each Tactic carries a number and a one-time or ongoing bonus; the
   player who picked the lowest number goes first, and going first is
   traded off against the strength of the bonus (the "Early Bird" Tactic
   guarantees first turn and grants nothing else). Turn order for the
   round is fixed by the chosen numbers once revealed.
3. Players take turns in that fixed order (not alternating — one full
   turn per player, then the next player's full turn, cycling until the
   round's end condition is met) — see §3.
4. At night, gold-mana dice in the Source are set to their depleted
   face at the start of the round; at day, black-mana dice are. (This
   models "gold is a day resource, black is a night resource" — see
   §4.)

There is no fixed number of turns per player per round; the round ends
when every player has ended their turn for that round (or, in
co-op/solo, when the dummy-player clock runs out — §9).

## 3. Turn structure

A player's turn has two parts, always in this order:

1. **Movement** — spend Move points (generated by playing cards) to step
   the Hero's figure across the hex map, one hex at a time, paying each
   hex's terrain cost. Moving onto an unrevealed hex's tile triggers
   exploration (§6).
2. **Action** — exactly one of: interact with a site (recruit, buy
   healing, plunder, etc., see §6), fight an enemy, or do nothing. A
   player may also skip movement and act in place, or move and then do
   nothing, or do nothing at all. **Resting** (§3.1) replaces both parts.

At the end of the turn the player draws back up to their hand limit (5,
increasing with level — see §7) from their Deed deck, discarding nothing
automatically; unplayed cards from the previous hand stay in hand rather
than being discarded.

### 3.1 Resting

Instead of a normal turn, a player may **Rest**: no movement, no action.
The player discards one non-Wound card from hand (or, if the hand is all
Wounds, none) and may discard any number of Wound cards from hand — the
discarded Wounds do not heal, they return to the general Wound pile and
can be redrawn later; Resting is how a hand clogged with Wounds gets
playable again without spending Healing points.

### 3.2 Playing a card

Every non-Wound Deed card can be played two ways:

- **For its printed effect** — the card's own basic (and, if paid for
  with mana, powered) text, see §5.
- **Sideways**, for a flat **1** of Move, Influence, Attack or Block —
  the player's choice of which of the four, regardless of what the
  card's own text says. This is the escape hatch that keeps a bad hand
  playable: any card is always worth *something*.

Wound cards can never be played, sideways or otherwise, and cannot
normally be discarded outside Resting — they exist purely to sit in hand
and count against the hand limit until healed away or discarded during a
Rest.

## 4. Card anatomy

Four kinds of Deed card, distinguished by frame color:

- **Basic Action cards.** Each Hero's personal starting 16 (their deck
  before any recruitment), one showing the Hero's own portrait. Two
  effects on the card: a **basic effect**, free to use, and a
  **stronger effect**, unlocked by paying one mana of the color printed
  on that half of the card. A card typically has the basic effect on
  top and the mana-powered stronger version below it, and both are
  always the *same kind* of effect (both Move, or both Attack, etc.) —
  the color requirement, not the effect type, is what the mana pays for.
- **Advanced Action cards.** 28 of them **[approximate — count sourced
  once]**, gold-framed, no Hero-shield icon, drafted from a shared
  offer during play rather than owned from the start. Same basic/powered
  shape as Basic Actions, generally stronger. The introductory scenario
  restricts play to Basic Actions only (cards "1–16").
- **Spell cards.** Violet-framed. Rather than one card with a
  basic/powered pair, a Spell has a **top effect and a bottom effect
  with different names and different jobs** — the bottom is the
  spell's "ultimate" version and (per UltraBoardGames) can only be cast
  during **Night** rounds. This is the one card type in the game whose
  two halves are named as genuinely different abilities rather than
  "weak/strong of the same thing."
- **Artifact cards.** Gold-framed, two different effects on one card;
  the second (in a distinct, darker text box) is a one-shot power that
  **destroys the Artifact** when used — the card leaves play as the
  cost of using its stronger half, rather than the mana payment every
  other powered effect uses.
- **Wound cards.** Not a deck the player owns cards from; entered into
  hand directly whenever the Hero (or a Unit) is wounded in combat, and
  they count against hand size like any other card while contributing
  nothing playable.

**So a single Deed card can mean up to four different things in a turn**,
matching the stub's framing: its own basic effect, its own mana-powered
effect, or — for either of those — played sideways instead of for text
at all, as a flat 1 of whichever of Move/Influence/Attack/Block the
situation needs. Advanced Actions and Spells add a further wrinkle
(Spells' two halves are named separately rather than being a weak/strong
pair of one effect), but the "basic vs. powered, or ignore the text and
play it as a generic 1" shape is uniform across Basic and Advanced
Action cards, which are the large majority of any deck.

## 5. Mana system

**The Source.** A shared pool of six-sided mana dice, sized by player
count: **players + 2** dice **[approximate — sourced from one BGG
thread; base-game solo appears to use 3 with no dummy player]**. Each
die shows one of six colors: four basic (Red — attack/fire, Blue —
block/ice, Green — move/nature, White — influence/speed, per the classic
color-to-verb mapping) plus two special (Gold, Black). During a turn a
player may take one live (non-depleted) die from the Source as one mana
of its shown color, to pay for a powered card effect or a cost; the die
is not returned until the *end of the round*, when all Source dice are
returned and rerolled for the next round.

**Day/night legality.** Gold is usable only by Day; Black only by
Night. At the start of a Day round, black dice showing in the Source are
flipped to their depleted face (unusable that round); at the start of a
Night round, gold dice are depleted the same way. Basic colors (Red,
Blue, Green, White) are legal at any time. A rule of thumb sourced from
UltraBoardGames — "at least half the dice should show basic colors" —
governs the color distribution printed on the dice themselves, not a
per-round check.

**Black mana amplifies rather than merely paying.** Per the Game
Elements source, Black mana "amplifies the power of basic mana colors"
— i.e. it is not simply a fifth interchangeable color but has effects
beyond substituting for a basic color. The exact amplification rule
(likely: casting a Spell with Black instead of its own color produces a
stronger effect) was not independently confirmed this session —
**[unverified, check before building]**.

**Crystals.** A player may bank mana as physical **Crystal tokens** in
their inventory instead of spending a die's color immediately — a
Crystal is not time-limited the way a Source die is (it does not expire
at round end) but is capped at **3 crystals per color** per Hero. There
are 12 tokens of each basic color and 3 of each special color in the
supply overall **[approximate — sourced once]**. Crystals also count
directly toward one of the end-of-scenario scoring categories (§8).

Certain terrain (Crystal Mines) and sites grant a mana token or crystal
directly for ending a turn there, decoupled from the Source entirely —
see §6.

## 6. Map construction and site types

**The map is built during play, not declared complete at setup.** A
scenario starts with a handful of pre-placed tiles (typically the
portal/starting tile plus its immediate neighbors); every other tile
starts as a face-down marker and is **revealed and placed the first
time a Hero moves onto it**, turning what was an edge of the known map
into new hexes and new (initially hidden) sites. This is the
"fog-of-war, grown as you explore" property the stub calls out
specifically — see Stage 2's gap table for what this costs.

**Tile shape.** Each physical Map tile is a large hexagon made of **7**
small hex spaces (one center hex plus a ring of six) **[reasonably
confident — sourced from two independent hits]**. There are two decks
of tiles: roughly **11 Countryside tiles**, simpler and thinned out at
lower player counts (fewer players draw from a smaller countryside
pool — remove 3 at 2 players, 2 at 3 players, 0 at 4), and roughly **8
Core tiles**, harder, drawn once the Countryside deck runs dry, carrying
stronger enemies (Draconum) and City sites. **[both counts approximate,
sourced once each]**

**Ending the scenario by exploration.** Revealing a tile that contains a
**City** is (in many scenarios, including the introductory one) the
scenario's own end trigger, independent of the round limit: once a City
tile is revealed, every player gets exactly one more turn and the
scenario ends and is scored.

### Site types and what interacting with them does

| Site | Category | What happens there |
|---|---|---|
| **Village** | inhabited, unfortified | Spend Influence (generated by playing cards) to **recruit a Unit** from the shared Unit offer (cost is the Unit's printed Influence cost; needs a spare Command token, see §7) or to **buy Healing** points. Villages can also be **plundered**: -1 Reputation, in exchange for drawing 2 cards from your own Deed deck — not an action, and usable only once per visit. |
| **Monastery** | inhabited, unfortified | Similar interaction site to a Village (recruit/heal), can alternatively be *burned down* for a large Reputation penalty (**-3**) — an aggressive, non-default option rather than the normal use of the site. |
| **Keep** | fortified | Cannot be entered or passed through without fighting its garrison. Moving onto its hex forces an **assault**: movement ends immediately, combat is fought against the (randomly drawn, gray) garrison enemy token, which is treated as **fortified** (see §7 combat rules) whether or not an owner is present. Assaulting costs **-1 Reputation** regardless of outcome. Win, and the Hero **conquers the Keep** — becomes its owner, gaining ongoing benefits (and it now counts as a per-scenario scoring shield, §8) — but if the garrison was fought rather than a rival player's forces, victory Fame is only **half, rounded up**. |
| **Mage Tower** | fortified | Same assault mechanics as a Keep — fortified enemy, -1 Reputation to attempt, conquerable. |
| **City** | fortified, scenario-ending | The largest fortified site; assaulting one is a bigger fight (multiple defenders, per the scenario), and revealing a City tile in the first place is frequently the scenario's own end trigger (see above), independent of whether that City is ever actually conquered. |
| **Dungeon** | adventure site, unfortified to enter but hides an enemy | Entering triggers a fight against a face-down enemy token. Win, and the site is marked with the Hero's Shield token (a scoring marker, §8) and the enemy discarded; the Hero also claims a reward at end of turn — roll to determine a **Spell** (on a Gold or Black result) or an **Artifact** (any other color). |
| **Ruins** | adventure site | Draw and read the tile's specific Ruins description card — effects vary per Ruin and are not a single fixed rule. |
| **Monster Den / Spawning Grounds** | adventure site, rampaging | Home to a **rampaging** enemy that can be provoked into combat by moving adjacent; unlike fortified sites this does not block movement outright. Losing enemy tokens here are returned rather than discarded (they can reappear). |
| **Magical Glade** | terrain feature, not a "site" proper | Ending a turn here lets a Hero discard one Wound card for free, or (once per visit, colour depending on day/night) gain a free Gold (day) or Black (night) mana token, decoupled from the Source. |
| **Crystal Mine** | terrain feature | Ending a turn here grants one Crystal of the mine's color, subject to the same 3-per-color cap as any other Crystal gain. |

## 7. Fame, Reputation, and leveling

**Fame** is a single running score, tracked on a numbered track,
gained primarily by winning combats (each enemy token prints a Fame
value awarded on defeat) and by some scenario objectives. Reaching Fame
thresholds advances a Hero's **level** (maximum level 10). Per
UltraBoardGames, level 2 needs "at least 3 Fame," level 3 needs "a
total of 8 Fame"; the full threshold table for every level was not
independently confirmed — **[unverified past level 3, get the actual
table before implementing a level-up curve]**. Leveling alternates in
what it grants: on even levels the Hero learns a new **Skill** (a
passive/activatable Hero-specific power, not modeled in detail by any
source consulted) and a new Advanced Action card (added to their deck);
on odd levels the Hero's base stats (Armor, hand limit) improve and a
new **Command token** is unlocked, raising how many Units they may
control simultaneously. Hand limit is 5 at low levels, rising to 6 at
level 5 **[sourced once]**.

**Reputation** is a second, separate track, centered on 0, moved by
specific triggers — assaulting a fortified site (-1), burning a
Monastery (-3), plundering a Village (-1), defeating certain enemies
(+1), and other scenario/card-specific triggers. Reputation modifies
**Influence**: a positive Reputation position adds a bonus to Influence
generated when interacting with a site, a negative one subtracts, and
the modifier applies **once per turn** regardless of how many things are
bought that turn. The track's worst space is marked with a red **X**:
landing there means the locals refuse to deal with the Hero at all —
**no interaction of any kind** is possible until Reputation improves.
Reputation, notably, has **no direct effect on combat** per the sources
consulted — it is purely a social-standing modifier on Influence and
site access.

## 8. End of scenario and scoring

A scenario ends on whichever trigger it declares — most commonly
revealing a City tile (§6) or hitting the scenario's round limit — after
which every remaining player gets exactly one final turn before the game
is scored.

**Final scoring adds bonus Fame across six categories**, each comparing
players against each other (the player with the highest total in a
category gets an extra flat bonus, typically **+3**):

- **Greatest Knowledge** — 2 Fame per Spell owned, 1 per Advanced
  Action owned.
- **Greatest Loot** — 2 Fame per Artifact owned, 1 Fame per every 2
  Crystals held.
- **Greatest Leader** — Fame equal to the sum of owned Units' levels
  (a Wounded Unit counts at half value).
- **Greatest Conqueror** — 2 Fame per Shield token placed on a
  conquered Keep, Mage Tower or Monastery.
- **Greatest Adventurer** — 2 Fame per Shield token placed on a
  cleared adventure site (Dungeon, etc.).
- **Greatest Beating** (a penalty, not a bonus) — lose 2 Fame per
  Wound card still sitting in the Deed deck at game end; whoever loses
  the most this way loses an *additional* 3.

Whoever has the highest final Fame total wins — in the base introductory
scenario this **is** the win condition; other scenarios in the book
layer their own specific objectives (conquer specific sites, control
specific regions, solve a mystery) on top of or instead of a Fame race.
The published scenario book has roughly 11 base-game scenarios spanning
competitive, cooperative and solo modes **[approximate, not
independently itemized]**.

## 9. Solo and cooperative scenario structure

**This is the mode this repository would realistically target** —
ravel's stated non-goals rule out real-time play and player-to-player
negotiation/bluffing (`AUTHORING.md`'s "rules that do not fit"), and
Mage Knight's *competitive* mode ("Conquest") is exactly a race governed
by simultaneous, private Tactic-card selection every round plus indirect
map competition for the same fortified sites — reachable in principle
(nothing about it needs real-time or a negotiation channel) but adds
several extra moving parts a cooperative/solo scenario does not need.
**Note the distinction clearly and do not aim at Conquest as the first
target.**

- **Solo play** uses the same rules as multiplayer with one Hero and,
  per one BGG thread, does **not** use a dummy player at all in the base
  game — the base game's solo scenarios are simply a single Hero played
  against the scenario's round limit and objective, with a smaller
  Source (3 dice, i.e. players + 2 with 1 player) **[approximate]**.
  (A genuine AI opponent — "Volkare" — belongs to the *Lost Legion*
  expansion, not the base game, and should not be assumed available.)
- **Cooperative play** (2+ Heroes) shares a single win/lose condition
  across all players rather than each scoring independently — the
  scenario states an objective (conquer a set of cities, clear a
  region, survive to a round limit) that the whole table either
  achieves or does not.
- **The "dummy player"** is the base game's clock mechanic for
  scenarios that want time pressure without a second real opponent: a
  separate small deck is discarded from at a fixed rate each round, and
  when that deck is exhausted the round (or the scenario) ends,
  regardless of what the real players have or haven't finished. It is
  **not an AI** — it makes no decisions, occupies no map space, and
  fights no combats. It exists purely to bound how many turns the real
  players get, which is a much smaller thing to implement than an
  opponent.
- Round and turn structure (§2–§3), the mana system (§5), combat (§7 —
  actually §"combat" was folded into resistance/assault above; see §6
  for fortified-site fights) and map exploration (§6) are all
  unchanged in solo/co-op — the only things that differ from
  competitive play are the win condition (shared rather than raced) and
  the presence/absence of the dummy-player clock.
