# The Crew: The Quest for Planet Nine — Rules

Thomas Sing, KOSMOS, 2019. Cooperative trick-taking, 3–5 players (a 2-player
variant exists, §9), 50 sequential missions in a shared campaign logbook.

**Sources.** Primary: the [official English rulebook PDF](https://cdn.1j1ju.com/medias/8f/13/09-the-crew-the-quest-for-planet-nine-rulebook.pdf)
(also mirrored on [BoardGameGeek's filepage](https://boardgamegeek.com/filepage/194487/official-english-rules-from-the-publisher)),
fetched and read in full (22 pages) — every unattributed quote below is
verbatim from it. The [official mission logbook PDF](https://cdn.1j1ju.com/medias/dd/5a/3a-the-crew-the-quest-for-planet-nine-log-book.pdf)
was also fetched and read directly (missions 1–36 of 50), and is the source
for §4's task-type variety — the actual printed text of specific missions,
not a paraphrase. Secondary, for the sequel comparison only (§10):
[Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crew_(card_game)),
[UltraBoardGames' rules page](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/the-crew/game-rules.php),
[GeekyHobbies' rules writeup](https://www.geekyhobbies.com/the-crew-the-quest-for-planet-nine-card-game-review-and-rules/),
and search-result framing of the *Mission Deep Sea* rulebook. Anything not
confirmed from the two primary PDFs is flagged inline and collected in §11.

**One correction to flag up front, since it changes how §4 has to be read.**
The task assigned by the objective's own framing — "win exactly N tricks,"
"win no tricks," "win the last trick," relative-order tasks — describes the
*campaign's* task variety accurately, but not the *task deck's*. The
physical 36-card task deck expresses exactly one thing: "win the trick
containing this specific numbered card," optionally sequenced against other
task cards by an order token. Every other task shape — no tricks, exactly N
tricks, the last trick, a trick with every card in a named set, a running
cap on how far apart two crew members' trick counts may drift — is **not**
a task card at all. It is bespoke rule text printed on that mission's page
in the logbook, assigning the condition directly to a named or
commander-chosen crew member, with no card changing hands for it. Both
systems coexist and can appear in the same mission; §4 covers both and says
which is which.

## 1. Card composition and dealing

**40 large "playing" cards**: 36 **color cards** in **four colors — pink,
blue, green, yellow — values 1–9** each, plus **4 rocket cards, values
1–4**. Confirmed directly from the Game Materials page and cross-checked
against every worked example in the rulebook's own trick illustrations
(pink 1/5/9, blue 3/7, green 3/5/9, yellow 2/6/8, rocket 1–4 all appear by
name).

**36 small task cards**, one per non-rocket color card (4 colors × 9 = 36 —
there is no rocket task card; rockets can never be assigned as a task, see
§6). **5 large reminder cards**, **16 tokens** (10 task-order tokens, 5
radio communication tokens, 1 distress-signal token), **1 commander token**,
1 rulebook/logbook.

**Dealing** (Mission Preparation, step 1): *"Shuffle the 40 large playing
cards and distribute them face down and equally to all crew members. If
there are three of you playing, one person will get one more card than the
rest. After the last trick, the card that remains unplayed."*

| Players | Hand size | Tricks per mission |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 13 for two players, 14 for one (40 = 13+13+14) | 13 (the 14th player's extra card is never played) |
| 4 | 10 each | 10 |
| 5 | 8 each | 8 |

Only 3 divides unevenly; 4 and 5 both divide 40 exactly. The rulebook does
not say which seat gets the extra card at 3p beyond "one person" — treated
as an ordinary consequence of the deal, not a fixed seat rule.

## 2. Trick resolution, in exact order

1. **Who leads.** The **commander** — whoever was dealt the rocket 4 for
   this mission attempt (see §5) — leads the first trick of the mission.
   *"Once all the tasks have been distributed, the commander starts the
   first trick. And then for the remainder of the mission attempt, the
   person who won the previous trick always starts the next trick."*
   Missions that carry no task cards still use this rule verbatim: *"Some
   missions do not use task cards. During these missions, the commander
   will start the first trick."*
2. **Play order.** Clockwise from whoever leads, one card per player,
   played face up into the center: *"in turn order each player plays one of
   his or her own cards face up in the middle of the table. This playing of
   one card by each is called the 'trick.'"*
3. **Follow suit if able.** *"Following suit means that each player must
   'follow' the suit choice of the first player; i.e., you have to play a
   card of the same type. If you do not have a card of this suit you may
   play a card of a different suit."* No mandate on *which* legal card:
   *"During a trick, there is no mandate to play a specific card. If you
   have multiple cards that you can play, you may play a low card, even
   though you would have been able to win the trick with a higher card...
   you are not forced to win the trick."*
4. **Rockets are trump, unconditionally — with one precise wrinkle.**
   *"The rocket cards are trump cards. This means that they always win the
   trick, no matter which other cards are in play."* Trump is not "above a
   certain rank" — every rocket (1–4) beats every color card regardless of
   value, always. The wrinkle: **rocket is also a followable suit in its
   own right.** *"During a color-suit trick, a rocket may only be played if
   a card in the color suit cannot be played. If a rocket card is played
   first, this will establish the suit and must be followed if possible."*
   So: if a rocket is *led*, players holding a rocket must play one if they
   have it (same follow-suit rule, just with rocket as the led suit); if a
   *color* is led, a rocket may only be played by someone with no card of
   that led color (rockets are never legal "instead of following," only as
   the off-suit escape).
5. **Winner.** *"The card with the highest numeric value wins the trick,
   but only if that player has followed suit... the trick can only be won
   with cards that have followed suit. The card with the highest numeric
   value in the designated suit wins the trick."* If any rocket was played,
   the highest rocket in the trick wins outright, over every color card
   however high — *"If several rockets are in contention, the one with the
   highest value wins."* Off-suit cards played only because a player
   couldn't follow (the rulebook's "any card of your choice" case) never
   contend for the win, whatever their printed value — the rulebook's own
   worked example: a green 3 leads, a yellow 9 is played off-suit later in
   the trick, and *"[the green] 5 is higher and thus wins the trick.
   Because even though [the yellow] 9 had the highest value it did not
   follow suit and thus cannot win."*
6. **What happens to the cards.** *"A trick that has been won is set aside
   face down. You may only take a look at the cards from the trick won most
   recently."* The trick's winner is the one who sets it aside — **which
   player physically collects a trick has no scoring consequence by
   itself**: nothing in the rules reads "your own tricks won" as a pile to
   count or score by size. What matters is only whether a specific *card*
   the group needed appeared in a trick a specific *player* won (§4) — the
   trick-winner identity is checked once, against outstanding tasks, then
   the whole trick is set face down and forgotten except as the most-recent
   one browsable.

## 3. Communication token

Each player has **exactly one radio communication token, usable exactly
once per mission attempt** — not per trick: *"Each of you has one radio
communication token, which can be used exactly once per mission. It may be
used at any time before a trick, but never during it. If you fail in a
mission attempt, you may of course use it again during the next
attempt."* ("Mission attempt" matters: each retry of a failed mission is a
fresh shuffle and fresh deal — see §7 — and the token refreshes with it.)

**Procedure.** Take one of your own color cards (never a rocket — *"Rocket
cards can never be communicated!"*) and lay it face up in front of you.
Place the communication token on it, green side up, in one of three
positions, and exactly one of the three conditions must be true of that
card or it may not be chosen at all:

- **Top = highest** card of that color in your hand.
- **Middle = only** card of that color in your hand.
- **Bottom = lowest** card of that color in your hand.

Worked example from the rulebook confirms a card can fail all three and
therefore be uncommunicable: *"6 cannot be communicated, as it is neither
the highest nor the lowest, nor is it the only yellow card in the hand."*
After placing the card and token, take one of the 5 **reminder cards** into
your hand as a physical marker that a communicated card is still live on
the table; when you eventually play the communicated card, put the reminder
card back down in the trick and flip the token to its **red (used) side.**

**No take-backs, ever.** *"The placement of the radio communication tokens
may not be changed if the statement is no longer correct... Nevertheless,
the radio communication token may not be moved."* A communicated "highest"
that later becomes merely "the only one left" (because the others were
played) stays exactly where it was placed — the group must reason from
stale-but-honest information, never a live one.

No other communication is permitted at all, in either direction: *"You
cannot talk about the cards in your hand. You are not allowed to show, say
or suggest to other crew members which cards you have."*

## 4. Task types — the standard deck and the per-mission special rules

### 4a. The standard 36-card task deck

One task card per non-rocket color card (a task card literally names a
color and number, e.g. "pink 1"). *"A task is fulfilled when a crew member
wins a trick containing the playing card matching one of his or her task
cards."* Multiple of a player's own tasks can complete in the same trick if
that trick happens to contain more than one of their target cards. A task
is turned face down in front of its owner the instant it's fulfilled.

**Order tokens** (10 total) layer a sequencing requirement on top of two or
more task cards a mission specifies, drawn from the logbook's own legend:

| Token | Means |
|---|---|
| `1` / `2` / `3` / `4` / `5` | Must be fulfilled **first** / **second** / **third** / **fourth** / **fifth**, among the tokened tasks only |
| `Ω` | Must be fulfilled **last** |
| `›` | Must be fulfilled **before** `››` |
| `››` | Must be fulfilled **after** `›` |
| `›››` | Must be fulfilled **after** `››` |

Numbered tokens name a strict position among *only the tasks that carry a
number token* — *"The task token 1 does not mean that the task must be
fulfilled in the first trick — it only has to be fulfilled first out of all
the pending tasks"* [with a token]. Arrow tokens are a partial order
(before/after), looser than a strict numbering: *"the pink 1 is won before
the blue 6, between the blue 6 and the green 3, or is won after the green
3, does not matter"* as long as blue-before-green holds. **Violating a task
order condition is an immediate mission loss** (§6).

### 4b. Special mission rules — not task cards at all

Confirmed directly from reading the printed text of missions 1–36 in the
logbook (bold-highlighted rule text is the mission's own convention for "a
special rule, read carefully"). Every one of these assigns its condition
without dealing a task card for it:

- **Win no tricks at all**, assigned to a specific (sometimes
  commander-chosen) player — mission 5: *"The sick crew member may not win
  a single trick."* Mission 16/17 generalize the same shape to *cards*
  rather than a player: *"You cannot win a trick with a 9-value card"* —
  nobody, all mission — sustained across two consecutive missions.
- **Win exactly N tricks** — mission 33: *"The selected crew member must
  win exactly 1 trick, but not with a Rocket card."*
- **Win the first and/or last trick** — mission 34: *"Your Commander must
  win the first and last trick."* (First trick is already guaranteed to the
  commander by the base rule; this mission additionally requires they
  *win* it, and also the last.)
- **Win a trick with every card in a named small set** — mission 13: *"You
  must win a trick with each Rocket card"* (four separate winning tricks,
  one per rocket value, not necessarily the same player). Mission 26 is the
  same shape with a count: *"Two 1-value cards must win one trick each."*
- **A running parity constraint across the whole crew**, checked
  continuously rather than once at the end — missions 29 and 34: *"At no
  time may a crew member have won 2 tricks more than another crew
  member."* This is the one task-shaped rule that cannot be checked
  end-of-mission only; it must be evaluated after every trick throughout
  the attempt.
- **Assignment via secret yes/no polling ("Commander's decision")** —
  mission 5, and documented generally on rulebook p.17: task cards (or, as
  in mission 5, a special condition) are placed face down; each player is
  asked privately whether they'd be willing to take on *all* of them,
  answerable only "Yes" or "No"; the commander alone decides who actually
  gets the assignment from among the willing.
- **Even, un-negotiated assignment ("Commander's distribution")** —
  missions 20, 27, 32, 36 (*"Your Commander distributes the individual
  orders"* / *"determines who receives the orders"*): no open draft at all
  — task cards are drawn one at a time and the commander alone decides, in
  secret, who receives each, subject only to the rule that *"the tasks must
  be evenly distributed: at the end of the distribution, no one may have
  two tasks more than another crew member."*

Two further mission-only conditions, unrelated to tasks but part of the
same "special rule" vocabulary and worth naming since they interact with
communication (§3) and turn order:

- **Dead zone / disruption**: communication is restricted to
  highest-or-lowest-or-only *without* placing the token (a "guess from
  context" mode), or suspended entirely until a named trick number.
- **A forced pass of one hand card to a neighbor** before the mission
  starts (the distress-signal help mechanic, always optional, §8) — not a
  task, but changes the deal every crew member actually holds.

## 5. Task distribution procedure

Confirmed as an exact sequence from rulebook p.8–9:

1. Shuffle the 36 task cards; reveal the number the mission specifies
   (shown as a small numeral icon on the mission page) face up in the
   center of the table. Every other task card stays out of play for that
   mission entirely.
2. Every player looks at their own dealt hand of playing cards (§1).
   **Whoever holds the rocket 4 announces it and becomes the commander**
   for this mission attempt, taking the commander token as a physical
   reminder. *"Whoever has the four rocket gets the commander token for the
   current mission attempt. The commander is still a member of the crew."*
3. **The commander picks first**, taking one revealed task card and
   placing it face up in front of themselves. *"Your commander will always
   be the first to choose a task card from the center of the table."*
4. **Then every other player takes one task card in turn, clockwise,
   one at a time, until the shared pool is empty.** *"Each crew member
   selects a task card one at a time until all the tasks have been
   distributed. Each player could get several tasks and not all players
   will necessarily have the same number of tasks."* (No stated cap on how
   many rounds of picking happen — the loop just continues clockwise,
   starting from the commander, until the revealed pool is exhausted; with
   more tasks than players, the second pass again starts back at the
   commander.)

Two mission-specific variants replace step 4's open choice: **Commander's
decision** (a private yes/no poll, then the commander alone assigns) and
**Commander's distribution** (the commander assigns every task in secret,
one at a time, bound only by the "nobody gets two more than anyone else"
evenness rule) — both described in §4b, both still start from "commander
first" as the organizing principle.

**Choosing which task to take is a genuine decision informed by your own
hand** — the rulebook's own worked example (mission 18) has the commander
picking the pink 1 specifically *because* he already holds the pink 9 in
hand, making that task easy, and a later player forced into a yellow task
he holds *no* yellow cards for at all, by elimination.

## 6. Mission win/loss conditions

**Win**: every distributed task is fulfilled, with no order violation and
no impossible-task loss ever triggering along the way. *"As soon as each
one of you have completed your own tasks, you have completed the mission
successfully and can start the next mission immediately."*

**Instant loss, provable and directly observable — not deduced.** Two
independent triggers, both checked the moment a trick resolves, neither
requiring any reasoning about cards still hidden in other players' hands:

1. **Wrong-player card loss.** *"If a player wins even a single playing
   card for which another player has the corresponding task card, you lose
   immediately and have to restart the mission from the beginning."* This
   is the objective's "task becomes provably impossible" case, and the real
   rule is sharper than "the required card left play unclaimed": the
   moment *any* trick is won, every card in it is checked against every
   *other* player's outstanding tasks — if one of them matches, the game
   ends right there. The task holder never gets a chance to "still win it
   later," because by definition the card has already been won, by the
   wrong person, in the trick that just closed.
2. **Task order violation.** *"Violating the task order condition will
   immediately cause the mission to fail."* Checked at the same moment: a
   task tokened `2` completing before whichever task carries `1` still sits
   open, or a `››`-tokened task completing before its paired `›` has, is
   an instant loss on the spot.

**On loss**: *"For each new attempt, we reshuffle all the cards and deal
each crew member new cards, and receive new tasks."* A failed mission is
retried from a fresh shuffle, not resumed — the logbook records the number
of attempts a crew needed, not whether any single attempt got close.

**Campaign structure.** 50 missions, played in sequence by default and
increasing in difficulty, but not required to be strictly sequential: *"you
can also play the missions out of order if that is your preference."* Each
completed mission is entered in the logbook (attempt count, and whether the
optional distress-signal help was used, §8) and the crew moves on to the
next; nothing about a mission's outcome — beyond "did you finish it" and
"how many tries did it take" — carries forward as game state into the next
mission. Each mission is, mechanically, a complete fresh game: new shuffle,
new deal, new task distribution, new commander (whoever holds the rocket 4
in *that* deal, not necessarily the same player as last time).

## 7. Difficulty and the distress-signal help mechanic

The 50 missions are ordered by intended difficulty, and the rulebook is
explicit that this scales sharply with player count: *"With four players,
in the first mission alone there are over 42 trillion possible ways that
the cards can be distributed."* An optional per-mission help: before any
communication happens (after dealing, before the first trick), the crew may
activate a **distress signal** — each player passes exactly one hand card
to a chosen neighbor, same direction for everyone, rockets exempt from
being passed. Using it increases that mission's logged attempt count by one
regardless of outcome, and every mission can be completed without it.

## 8. Playing with two or five players

**Two players** (rulebook p.19, a genuine variant, not the default 3–5):
rockets are set aside; the other 39 cards are dealt into a face-down row of
7 with 7 more face-up on top (a simulated **third hand, "JARVIS"**, an AI
represented by a double row of 14 cards); rockets are then shuffled back
into the remainder and the two real players get 13 cards each. The
commander (whoever holds rocket 4) plays *both* their own cards and every
JARVIS card face-up, deciding JARVIS's play with no discussion allowed;
only JARVIS's currently-face-up card in each pile is legal to play, and the
card beneath is revealed only once its trick fully resolves.

**Five players** (from mission 25 onward, marked by a gold mission-number
border in the logbook): one additional optional rule — *"one crew member
can hand over his or her own task card to another crew member"* after
tasks and cards are dealt but before the first trick, keeping the total
task count fixed (one player ends up with one more task, another with one
fewer; no returning a task to the shared stock).

## 9. Colorblind accessibility

Each of the four suit colors also carries a shape symbol (circle, triangle,
square, X) printed on the cards, for players who cannot reliably
distinguish the four colors — purely presentational, no rules consequence.

## 10. The sequel — Mission Deep Sea (2021), briefly

Not the subject of this document, but worth naming what actually changed,
since a careless assumption here would leak into Stage 2. Confirmed via
secondary sources (not independently fetched from a primary rulebook, so
treated as **[medium-confidence]**): submarine cards replace rockets as the
trump suit (same "always wins, highest submarine breaks ties" behavior);
the 36-card fixed task deck is replaced by 96 task cards each printed with
three difficulty ratings (for 3/4/5 players), drafted face-up until their
combined rating hits the mission's target number rather than a fixed count;
32 new missions; task types explicitly include "win exactly N tricks,"
"win no tricks," and "win the first/last trick" as *printed task-card text*
rather than mission-logbook special rules — meaning the sequel folded
exactly the task variety this document found scattered through the base
game's logbook special rules (§4b) into the task-card system itself. This
is the one place the two games differ structurally rather than just in
content volume, and it's a plausible explanation for why the objective's
framing described task variety as if it lived on cards: in the sequel, it
does.
